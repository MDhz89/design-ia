# Brand hierarchy and composition

## Objective

Make the product feel branded, balanced, and intentionally composed without
turning every section into a logo display or forcing equal attention onto every
element.

## Audit brand assets before layout

Inspect the project for:

- Full wordmark.
- Isotype or symbol.
- Horizontal and vertical lockups.
- Light and dark variants.
- Monochrome variants.
- Existing clear-space and color usage.

Choose the asset according to the available space:

- Use a horizontal wordmark or lockup in wide navigation.
- Use an isotype only when the brand remains recognizable without its name.
- Use the full mark where brand recall matters and enough space exists.
- Use the appropriate light or dark asset instead of applying arbitrary filters.

Do not shrink a detailed or circular logo until it becomes a decorative dot. If
the needed variant is missing, identify it as an asset requirement instead of
reconstructing or distorting the logo.

## Brand presence across the page

Create a restrained brand rhythm:

- Make the navigation mark immediately recognizable.
- Reinforce the brand near one meaningful product demonstration when natural.
- Restore brand presence near the final conversion point.

Do not repeat the logo in every section. Strategic recurrence should improve
recognition, not replace hierarchy or content.

Judge logo size by perceived legibility, not only pixel dimensions. Verify it at
the actual desktop and mobile render sizes with surrounding elements present.

## Color ownership

Assign explicit roles to brand colors:

- One dominant accent for primary actions, important states, and key emphasis.
- One secondary accent used sparingly for contrast, differentiation, or depth.
- Neutral colors for most surfaces, text, borders, and supporting content.

Do not distribute two brand colors equally by default. Equal use weakens
ownership and often creates a generic gradient-heavy appearance.

Use a brand-supported gradient only when it creates continuity across a small
number of high-value moments, such as a key phrase and related product evidence.
Do not apply gradients to every heading.

## Visual hierarchy

For every section, identify:

1. Primary message.
2. Supporting explanation.
3. Product evidence or action.
4. Orientation devices such as section numbers, labels, or badges.

Size and contrast must follow that order unless the product evidence itself is
the primary message.

Treat section numbers as orientation, not headlines. Treat prices, commissions,
percentages, and metrics as evidence whose prominence must match their role.
Reduce them when they compete with the proposition or brand; increase them only
when the commercial decision depends on that value.

Do not enlarge an element merely because the layout has empty space.

## Reading order and evidence

For left-to-right languages, place the explanatory proposition before its
supporting visual evidence in both DOM and perceived reading order by default:

- Message or characteristic first.
- Product example, interface state, or diagram second.

Alternating layouts may still be used, but do not reverse the logical reading
order solely to create visual variety. On mobile, stack the message before the
evidence unless the evidence is required to understand the message.

## Sequential processes

When steps form a real sequence, communicate continuity rather than presenting
them as unrelated cards:

- Use a line, path, progress rail, shared baseline, or controlled transition.
- Mark the current or active step clearly.
- Keep step numbers subordinate to step titles.
- Preserve semantic ordered-list structure.
- Adapt horizontal timelines into readable vertical sequences on narrow screens.

Do not add connectors to items that are independent or can occur in any order.

## Plans and featured options

Emphasize a recommended plan using the lightest treatment that creates a clear
distinction:

- Brand-colored border.
- Restrained outer halo.
- Small badge.
- Slight surface contrast.
- Deliberate scale or elevation that does not distort comparison.

Avoid large white or fully inverted surfaces when they overpower the entire
pricing section. Ensure the recommended state remains obvious without glare,
excessive glow, or color alone.

The plan name, price, included value, and action must remain easier to read than
the “recommended” decoration.

## Final CTA composition

Use the final CTA to close both the commercial and brand narrative:

- Give the primary action visual priority.
- Use a legible brand mark when it reinforces trust or recall.
- Avoid reducing the logo to a minor footer ornament if the CTA is the main
  branded closing moment.
- Keep the logo, message, and action as three distinct hierarchy levels.

Do not let an oversized logo displace the CTA or make the section resemble an
advertisement disconnected from the product.

## Acceptance checks

- The brand is recognizable in navigation at desktop and mobile sizes.
- The selected logo variant fits its container without distortion or illegible
  detail.
- One brand color clearly owns primary emphasis.
- Secondary color use is restrained and intentional.
- Section numbers, metrics, commissions, prices, and badges do not compete with
  the intended message.
- Text and evidence follow a logical reading order on desktop and mobile.
- Sequential steps appear connected only when they are truly sequential.
- The featured plan is distinct without becoming the heaviest visual block.
- The final CTA reinforces brand recall while preserving action priority.
